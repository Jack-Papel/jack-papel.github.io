Writing is Fundamental to Development Teams

When I joined my first corporate development team in 2022, I figured my past programming experience would be sufficient for me to do well. After all, when I had previously been the lead developer of Immortal Devs, a small Minecraft modding organization, I learned all about how to develop software effectively: In order for my code to be understood, it had to be readable; For the codebase to be reliable and our developers to grow, we enforced code review; and in order to explain complex algorithms, I had to utilize multiple modes of writing—and these skills translated well. But I still found that there were plenty of things I didn’t yet understand. Knowing how to write in software development was only half the battle—I now needed to learn how to write in a development team.

One way to analyze writing in a development team is using a lens created by John Swales, who in his article The Concept of Discourse Community, introduces the titular concept of discourse communities. Firstly, Swales proposes that in constituting a discourse community, a “commonality of goal” is required, not just a “shared object of study” (220). This means that software development itself doesn’t constitute a discourse community. It has no particular goals in-and-of-itself—only to develop software. But, an individual dev team has plenty of auxiliary goals: typically, they want to make code that can be maintained easily; and as mentioned before, they want to create code that is understandable. To these ends, writing does more than provide a means of communication—the goals revolve around writing. It mediates the activity itself—one of our threshold concepts—it’s the primary way that developers turn their individual knowledge into shared, shaped actions: well-written and architected code. Being the medium of activity, writing is what constitutes a dev team: the pursuit of better code, and thus better writing, is the reason they exist.

In order to better analyze the goals and activities of dev teams, another lens by Donna Kain and Elizabeth Wardle is relevant: Activity Theory—from their article Activity Theory: An Introduction for the Writing Classroom—is a lens in which discourse communities are analyzed via the activities they participate in and are organized around. Part of this lens is analyzing the motives for an activity, which they separate into objects and outcomes—immediate and long term goals, respectively. As mentioned, most groups of software developers have immediate—object—goals of creating maintainable, correct software. But development teams also have longer term—outcome—goals: providing a valuable service to users; creating value for the company; and improving internal processes to those ends. To achieve those goals, they use a variety of tools—another aspect of the lens: they write specifications to ensure users’ needs are met, and internal documentation to streamline processes. Of course, time spent writing these auxiliary resources is less time spent writing code—so just as much as development teams need to reflect on their code, they also need to reflect on their processes.

Swales also proposes that discourse communities “[possess] one or more genres in the communicative furtherance of its aims” (221). Together with Kain & Wardle’s lens, code comments and code reviews are not just tools used to write better code, they are genres owned by the dev teams that use them—genres which help shape collaboration in the community: code reviews are the primary way of giving feedback; comments are the primary way to share knowledge. This directly relates to our threshold concept Writing is a Dynamic, Multimodal Technology for Making Meaning. To write code as part of a dev team, you need to integrate these different modes: you can’t just think about the code you’re writing—you must also consider the code review that comes after; if you’re writing code with complex logic, you might need to include a link to a visual aide or technical paper.

In An Introduction to Discourse Analysis, James Gee proposes the idea of “building tasks”—things which language defines, and through which language is defined. Consider one’s identity in a dev team. For example, a team may have a ‘distinguished engineer’—someone with a lot of experience and know-how—and when they are providing expertise or shaping infrastructural decisions, they take on that identity. Their confidence and knowledge signal their authority and create that identity. However, when they are chatting with coworkers, they’re speaking and acting as just another one of the devs—their identity as a distinguished engineer is only taken on when it is enacted through the way they use language. Similarly, the power of a team leader is only granted by the rest of the dev team’s language shaping that power and identity: trusting them to make major decisions, following their commands, and more.

To analyze the ways in which a development team changes as the introduction of artificial intelligence becomes more commonplace in the field, Gee and Swales’ lenses don’t paint a full picture. However, Kain & Wardle’s lens of Activity Theory is a particularly insightful lens to analyze the differences in workflow. Particularly, the way activity is mediated fundamentally changes—coding agents (artificial programmers) aren’t helped by certain tools. For example, often coding agents don’t have the capacity to view visual or spatial aides, or understand them in the same way humans do. The division of labor also changes, with individuals on the team doing much less manual programming, instead instructing their fleet of agents to write and submit the code. Teams may opt for code reviews to be done by AIs too, and many even have AI help developers with technical problems, rather than take up other developers’ time with their questions.

Identities, and thus the subjects of the activity, change too. Recently, at my software development co-ops I’ve had to think more about whether my coding agents met a feature’s requirements and wrote maintainable code rather than do it myself. In a sense, every developer on the team becomes a leader to their artificial team—“It’s no different than being a software developer who then moves on to lead a team of developers”, says Papel, “Now every developer is like that”.

Using these lenses, we see what a dev team is at its core: a community bound by discourse, motivated by common goals, propelled by the genres and tools it utilizes, and defined through its members’ language. Even when artificial intelligence fundamentally changes how work is done in a dev team, at its core, a dev team is still defined by the language and discourse that enacts it. Importantly, to work in a dev team, knowing how to write in software development isn’t sufficient: what I lacked in knowledge about working in a dev team wasn’t because I didn’t know how to write code effectively—I did; and it wasn’t because I hadn’t worked with others—I had. It was because I hadn’t had the experience of working in a corporate dev team before. To succeed in a dev team—or any discourse community—you have to learn how writing defines it.
