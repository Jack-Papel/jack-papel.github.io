Reflection on You Can Learn to Write in General and Writing Knowledge Transfers Easily

Preface

One of the first assignments in the class was to read two segments from the book Bad Ideas About Writing. The first segment was You Can Learn to Write in General by Elizabeth Wardle, and the second was Writing Knowledge Transfers Easily by Ellen Carillo.

Reflection

One of our threshold concepts this semester is “Reflection is Critical for Writers’ Development”, and Ellen Carillo’s segment Writing Knowledge Transfers Easily from Bad Ideas about Writing explains this idea well. In this segment, she contests the commonly–held belief that students who take a writing course will automatically transfer what they learned into future courses. The word automatically here is important—students certainly can transfer their knowledge, but only when the writing course enables them to by teaching them to practice metacognition—a fancy word for reflecting on what they are learning—“thinking about thinking”.

Another threshold concept of ours is “Writing Enacts Disciplinarity”, and Elizabeth Wardle’s segment You Can Learn to Write in General from the same book—maybe it’s clear from the title—is basically all about this threshold concept. This segment has a simple but important point: Despite common belief, there is no such thing as writing in general—as a writer you always take into account audience, genre, and all sorts of things that cannot be removed from context. This threshold concept is about that same thing—the syllabus describes this concept as “Writing is a way of joining … disciplinary conversations”.

Something I wondered when I was reading Carillo’s section was: It explains that metacognition in writing classes is helpful for transferring knowledge—but how does it compare when considering metacognition in the “sending” class versus only the "receiving" class? In other words, if you were to take writing course A before another course B, would metacognition be as effective in course B only compared to course A only—or would metacognition in course A and not B be more effective—or would doing it in both be even better? I suspect the answer is the latter, but I would be interested to see a study on this.

P.S.

Reading Carillo’s section also made me think about my own writing—particularly within the view of our threshold concept on reflection. Maybe this is a little bit off-topic, but I think it’s not completely irrelevant. It might be clear by reading my writing that I tend to use em-dashes vociferously, which for most of my academic life has not been a problem. However, as of recently, the zeitgeist has had its eyes locked on AI and particularly the way AI writes—and as you may be aware, one of the common indicators of AI writing is an overuse of em-dashes. This has caused some confusion sometimes when I write to my friends, as they think I am posting ChatGPT “wordslop”, so to speak, and decide not to read it. But, it has also had a positive effect, I think, where it has caused me to think more critically about how to integrate more personal stories into my writing in order to oppose this misunderstanding. I think that’s a pretty good example of how “Writing Is a Dynamic, Multimodal Technology for Making Meaning”—because I am writing digitally, my writing style has to adapt to a medium where word vomit is not just possible, but commonplace.

Works Cited

Wardle, Elizabeth. “You Can Learn to Write in General.” Bad Ideas About Writing, Digital Publishing Institute, Morgantown, West Virginia, 2017, pp. 30–33.

Carillo, Ellen. “Writing Knowledge Transfers Easily.” Bad Ideas About Writing, Digital Publishing Institute, Morganville, West Virginia, 2017, pp. 34–37.
