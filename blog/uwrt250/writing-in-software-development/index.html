Writing is Fundamental to Software Development

Six years ago, I took AP Computer Science—which had a reputation in my high school for being a difficult class—but I had been programming for more than seven years at that point, so I would often spend most of my class time helping my classmates with their code. But, despite my experience I was often completely unable to read their code. Variables which I would label sum, row, col, and input, they had labeled a, b, c, and d; multiple statements would be written on single lines; functions would be named func2 and f. It made it next to impossible to help them with their code, and if I asked them to explain a section they wouldn’t be able to tell me what it did either. My classmates’ code was, in a sense, unreadable—that’s because they were piecing together a program like it was made up of mathematical formulae. They didn’t value writing as an essential aspect of software development.

Often, when people are introduced to programming, especially colloquially, they are given the idea that it’s only accessible by highly technical people—nerds, so to speak. Samuel Pepperman, a student at the Rochester Institute of Technology puts it another way: “There's kind of a push to make [it] seem like … ‘Only smart people can code’, ‘It’s super advanced’, ‘It’s difficult’”—but this perception is harmful to those trying to join and effectively participate in the discipline, and may have been what led my classmates to write unreadable code. Over the years, I’ve heard from countless peers: if programming is so mathematical, then I thought my code should look like math, too. But, what my peers were neglecting was the writing part of writing code. According to Pepperman, writing is of critical importance to a software developer: “It’s the fundamental way to communicate something to someone else”. He argues that code isn’t just written for the computer—it’s written for other humans to read and iterate on. “[You should be able to] show a non-technical person the file [you’re] working on, and they can … get a general gist of what's happening”. Being such an essential tool, writing is crucial for those looking to join the discipline of programming. Practically speaking, in order to effectively participate in software development, you have to be a good writer first. As Pepperman puts it, “If we say communication is a fundamental aspect of being a good developer, then being good at writing is critical”.

The strategies developers use to get their point across are innumerable, but one of the most common techniques is to utilize multimodal composing. Often emphasized by introductory courses in programming is the practice of adding comments to your code: essentially, adding plain-text notes inside your code for other programmers (or your future self) to read. Pepperman says that generally, code should explain itself—but in the cases where it can’t, he makes sure to add “a lot of in-text documentation”. He continues, “I will just have a comment paragraph in the middle of my file if I need to.… Sometimes I'll even just put a comment at the end of every single line saying what I'm doing”. In rare cases, even that isn’t sufficient to explain a snippet of code, and developers turn to other visual and spatial modes: As a computational mathematics major, my code tends to deal with complex functions and esoteric formulae, so when I need to, I’ll often include a direct link to a Desmos graph that explains them; Pepperman, who often works on more technical projects, says “If I pull from [a whitepaper or technical paper], I will directly just put the link to it in the code”. Using multimodal writing in this way is a natural way for software developers to share meaning.

Unfortunately, when the meaning of a piece of code is obscured or critical context is missing, the program can often still run just fine. This fact has caused me—and surely every developer—many headaches when that code later fails, which is why many software companies have a mandatory peer review process. Truveta CISO Oscar Papel says that even this is fallible, however: “As a software developer grows… ironically they become less capable of receiving feedback … in many cases”. This is not dissimilar to an idea from Naming What We Know: Threshold Concepts in Writing Studies, in which Charles Bazerman writes “If [a person becomes] deeply embedded in a set of writing practices … they may then assume … that what they learn in that specific context are general rules … for effective writing” (37). Papel says that as people gain experience, they can sometimes become more dogmatic or prideful, and this can lead to a lack of reflection. But, to improve as a software developer, “the truly great software developers get past all that”.

Feedback shouldn’t only come from others, though—another important skill in software engineering is taking time to reflect on your own code. Pepperman says reflection is an important tool for a developer to grow, “A lot of times, I will try to imagine myself as the person that's going to be maintaining my code later when I'm writing it, and that'll show me a shortcoming I had”. Importantly, this is done while writing the code, not afterward. In a section from Bad Ideas about Writing, Laura Giovanelli explains, “Revision is not the thing writers do when they’re done writing. Revision is the writing” (104). Trying to patch unmaintainable code is nigh-impossible—much harder than just putting in the work up front. Pepperman says, “It is of critical importance to reflect on your own code as much as [someone] trying to maintain your code later [will]”.

With the recent advent of artificially-intelligent coding agents, the best practices around software development have been changing faster than ever before. Now that the author and audience of your code is an artificial agent rather than a human, your writing skills have to adapt. According to Papel, developers are having to rethink their processes “multiple times per week” as artificial agents become increasingly capable of reading and understanding code, and software companies promote its internal use more. But, despite these rapidly evolving practices in software development, writing has remained essential—in a world where “the computer can now write code many times faster than you can”, according to Papel, your ability to write is still fundamental to your ability to develop software—but your role has changed. “You now have to be really good at communicating well with a computer”, says Papel, “It’s no longer about being really good at a [programming] language”. Writing for other human developers is still essential, though—with developers able to create teams of artificial agents on-the-fly to complete a task, their communications with other developers simply shift focus from nitty-gritty code details to bigger-picture ideas like the code architecture, the product’s features, and its design.

Despite the many forces which seem to act against it—public misconceptions about programming; stubborn developers; and an unclear future with AI—writing remains essential to software development. From readable code being essential to participating in the discipline; to using multimodal composing to share meaning; to critical reflection skills and peer review being essential for a programmer’s improvement, it’s clear that writing permeates all aspects of software development—and to participate in the discipline, you have to understand how writing is done.
